Seaford has a municipal electric utility

The City of Seaford operates an electric department and says it supplies customers within city limits. DEMEC also identifies Seaford as a public-power community that owns and maintains its distribution system. This matters when you look for an outage contact or ask which utility program applies to an HVAC proposal. Sources: City of Seaford Electric Department and DEMEC’s Seaford profile.

Start with the utility name on your own bill. Do not assume that a Seaford mailing address, a nearby town or a ZIP code identifies the electric provider for a particular property. The municipal source establishes city service; it does not establish every surrounding address’s provider. If your bill identifies Delmarva Power, use that utility’s own service information rather than the city’s electric contacts. Delmarva Power company information describes its regional service; no Delmarva Power boundary or individual Seaford-area address has been verified for this guide.

For municipal customers, the city publishes an outage and electric-issues contact on its electric department page. Use the current official listing rather than treating an HVAC appointment request as an outage report. A utility interruption and an equipment fault are different questions; this guide cannot determine which applies to your home.

Plan for both heating and cooling, not a statewide average

The Delaware Climate Office describes the state as having cold winter temperatures, hot summers and precipitation throughout the year, with surrounding water moderating extremes. This is statewide context, not a Seaford weather-station normal or a calculation for your house. Source: Delaware Climate Office: Delaware’s Climate.

When comparing heat-pump proposals, ask how the design addresses your home’s winter heating requirement, summer cooling and humidity control. ENERGY STAR explains that correct sizing matters and asks homeowners to have the contractor verify the home’s load using Manual J. A state climate description is not enough to select equipment capacity or decide whether backup heat is needed. Source: ENERGY STAR heat-pump buying guidance.

Bring address-specific questions to the estimate

  • Which electric utility is shown on my bill, and who should answer utility-service questions?
  • What home measurements and heating/cooling load calculation support this proposal?
  • Which permit or inspection requirements apply at this street address, and who will confirm them with the authority having jurisdiction?
  • If a program or incentive is mentioned, who administers it and what current written rules apply to my utility account and proposed equipment?

These are preparation questions, not verified permit requirements or an eligibility determination.
