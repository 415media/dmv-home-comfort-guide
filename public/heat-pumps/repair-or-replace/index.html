Heat pump repair, replacement or a new installation?

Compare the questions to ask about diagnosis, assessment, proposals and warranties without a replacement recommendation or price promise.

Compare explanations, not just equipment age

Ask the contractor to separate three questions: what is wrong now, what repair would address it, and why replacement or a new installation is being proposed. This guide does not use an age limit, repair-cost percentage or promised saving to decide for you.

ENERGY STAR identifies recurring repairs and comfort problems as reasons to evaluate a system, but also notes that uneven temperatures or humidity can involve insulation, air sealing or ducts. A symptom is a reason for assessment, not proof that replacing the heat pump will solve it. Source: ENERGY STAR heating and cooling guidance.

Questions to distinguish the proposed options
OptionAsk the contractor
RepairWhat fault was identified? What would the repair address, and what uncertainty or unrelated work would remain?
ReplacementWhy is replacement proposed instead of repair? What assessment supports the new design?
New installation or conversionWhat equipment, ducts, controls or related work would change? Who evaluates each part of that scope?

Ask for the home assessment behind the proposal

ENERGY STAR recommends verifying system size with a Manual J heating-and-cooling load calculation. Ask the contractor to explain the calculation for your home and the selection of matched equipment, rather than treating the existing unit’s size as the answer. Its guidance also identifies duct condition and airflow as installation considerations. Source: ENERGY STAR: sizing and installation guidance.

  • Which home measurements and assumptions support the proposed heating and cooling capacity?
  • What did the assessment find about ducts and airflow, or why is a ductless approach being considered?
  • How will the system meet heating needs in local winter conditions, and what backup heat, if any, is proposed?
  • If retaining a furnace in a dual-fuel system, who specifies the controls and explains operation?
  • Who assesses electrical requirements, permits and related work, and where is that responsibility written?

These are questions for qualified professionals, not a sizing calculation, electrical instruction or a statement that every home needs backup heat.

Make proposals comparable before choosing

  • Written findings, repair alternatives and proposed equipment model numbers.
  • Included ducts, controls, electrical work, removal, permits and commissioning responsibilities.
  • Itemized charges, exclusions and approval process for additional work.
  • Manufacturer warranty documents and separate labor coverage, including exclusions and who handles claims.
  • Completion records, owner instructions and the proposed maintenance requirements.

If an incentive or financing offer is included, ask for the current program administrator’s rules and written terms. No eligibility, tax benefit, price or saving is established here. Keep the scope and warranty documents, and seek clarification or a second proposal if explanations conflict. Related guides: no heat, AC repair and maintenance.
