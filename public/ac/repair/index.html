AC repair: what to ask before a visit

Prepare a description of your AC concern and a written-estimate checklist in this unreviewed local draft. No diagnosis or referrals.

Describe the cooling problem without diagnosing it

A useful service note records what you noticed, not a guess about a failed part. From normal occupied areas, note when the concern began, whether it affects one room or the whole home, and whether cooling seems different at different times. If visible without opening equipment or entering an unsafe space, record the thermostat display or an already-visible error message. Do not deliberately recreate a fault to make a recording.

  • Which rooms feel uncomfortable, and is the concern temperature, humidity or both?
  • Does air seem to reach the usual occupied rooms?
  • Have you noticed water, ice, unusual sounds or repeated interruptions during ordinary use?
  • What previous service paperwork or equipment model information do you already have?

This is a suggested communication checklist, not a diagnostic test or an instruction to keep equipment running. Do not remove covers, handle refrigerant or attempt electrical work.

Ask what the assessment actually checked

ENERGY STAR’s contractor maintenance checklist includes controls, condensate drainage, coils, refrigerant and blower airflow. These are professional assessment topics, not homeowner repair steps. Ask which findings are relevant to your concern and how they support the recommended work. Source: ENERGY STAR Maintenance Checklist.

Uneven temperatures or humidity do not, by themselves, establish that a new AC unit is required. ENERGY STAR notes that comfort problems may also involve insulation, air sealing or ductwork. Ask what evidence distinguishes an equipment problem from an air-distribution or building problem. Source: ENERGY STAR guidance on comfort and replacement.

A written repair-estimate checklist

  • Finding: What was found, and what evidence or measurements support it?
  • Scope: Which specific work is proposed, and what is not included?
  • Authorization: What diagnostic charge applies, and how will extra work require approval?
  • Alternatives: Is there a repair option, further assessment or a reason to compare replacement?
  • Warranty: Which parts and labor terms apply, and who handles a return visit?
  • Completion: How will the contractor document what was done and the resulting operation?

These are comparison prompts, not mandatory contract terms or a price quote. Ask for unfamiliar terms to be explained before approving work. If replacement is proposed, use the repair-or-replace guide; for planned care, see maintenance-plan questions. This guide does not promise an appointment or connect a service call.
