No heat? Prepare for a heating-service conversation

Safety first: an alarm or suspected leak is not a routine service request

If a carbon-monoxide alarm sounds, move outside to fresh air immediately and call emergency services, the fire department or 911 from outside. Do not look for the source. Do not reenter until emergency responders give permission. CPSC also advises getting outside immediately if you suspect symptoms of CO poisoning, seeking prompt medical attention and reporting the concern. Source: CPSC Carbon Monoxide Questions and Answers.

If a fuel-gas alarm sounds, you smell gas or otherwise suspect a leak, leave the building immediately and take others with you. Do not operate lights, appliances or phones in the affected area. From a phone away from the building or area, call 911 or your fuel-gas provider; do not return until emergency responders or the provider say it is safe. Source: NFPA household propane and natural-gas guidance.

CO and fuel-gas hazards are different: CO has no smell, and a CO alarm is not a substitute for fuel-gas detection. NFPA explains the distinction in the same guidance. Do not use the absence of an odor to rule out CO.

Do not improvise fuel-burning heat

CPSC warns against using gas ranges or ovens to heat a home and against operating portable generators in or near enclosed spaces, including homes and garages. An open garage door does not make generator use there safe. Do not use this guide as a substitute for emergency instructions or your appliance manual. Source: CPSC CO prevention guidance.

After urgent safety concerns: prepare a heating-service conversation

If there is no suspected emergency, tell the contractor what heating system you believe you have, if known: a furnace, boiler, heat pump or an unfamiliar system. Use existing paperwork rather than opening equipment to identify it. Describe when the concern started, whether it affects all rooms and what an already-visible thermostat or error display shows.

Ask whether the contractor works on that system and serves your exact address, what a diagnostic visit includes, whether an appointment is actually available and which charges require approval. No response time, emergency attendance or provider coverage is promised by this guide.

Keep combustion, electrical and backup-heat assessment with a professional

For fuel-burning heating, ENERGY STAR’s professional checklist includes fuel connections, burner combustion and heat-exchanger inspection. These are not homeowner checks. For a heat pump, ask the contractor to explain the system configuration and any backup heat before discussing a replacement or changing controls. Sources: ENERGY STAR Maintenance Checklist and ENERGY STAR heat-pump guidance.

No ignition, fuel-valve, wiring, breaker-reset or safety-control procedure is supplied here. Ask for the diagnosis, alternatives and proposed work in writing. Use the heat-pump comparison guide if replacement is raised, and the maintenance guide for future care.
